Duration distribution & Store-and-forward

What this shows

Two clocks on the same Files, side by side (merged from the former Duration distribution and Store-and-Forward pages, 2026-09-05). The Duration distribution on the left is the whole trip of a File; the Dwell-time distribution on the right is one piece of that trip — the wait inside SecureTransport between the inbound leg completing and the outbound leg starting. A File slow on the left can be slow because of a large transfer, a slow partner on either leg, retries, or a long dwell; the right-hand histogram and the tables below isolate the last of those causes.

Duration distribution (left)

A histogram of the Files by wall-clock duration — from the first record start to the last record end, gaps and retry idle included — in seven bands: up to 100 ms, 1 s, 10 s, 1 min, 5 min, 30 min and beyond. Split off the Duration report 2026-09-03. Two views behind a button row: OK transfers (the default) counts delivered Files only; All transfers adds the failed ones, whose duration is how long they ran before giving up.

Files is the count in the band and Share its part of the scope’s Files. A From/To date range re-aggregates both from the per-day buckets each row carries. In the All view the failed transfers — mostly instant 0-byte attempts — pile up in the first band.

Store-and-forward (right, and the tables below)

SecureTransport is a store-and-forward hub: a file arrives on an inbound leg, is held, then goes out on an outbound leg. This report measures the gap in between — how long each file waited before being forwarded — which nothing else on the site captures (Transfer Duration measures the end-to-end span, not the wait between legs). Two views:

  • Dwell-time distribution — how the waits are spread (under 1s, 1–5s, 5–30s, 30–60s, 1–5 min, over 5 min). Most files forward almost immediately; the tail is where latency lives.
  • Dwell by subscription — the average and maximum dwell per subscription, slowest-first, so you can see which flows queue.
  • Gap per day (absorbed from the former Inbound / Outbound Gap report, 2026-07) — per calendar day the Min / Median / Max and P25–P99 percentiles of the in→out gap, for the delivered Files with exactly one Inbound and one Outbound leg (gap = outbound start − inbound end, banked under the inbound date; the per-day stats are not additive, so a narrowed range blanks their totals).

How the dwell is computed

All the records for one logical transfer share a CoreId and sit together in the cache. For each CoreId the dwell is the earliest outbound start minus the latest inbound completion (the inbound row's end timestamp). A transfer is "measurable" only if it has both an inbound completion and an outbound start; a few transfers whose outbound leg starts before the inbound finished (overlapping/streamed legs) show a negative gap and are excluded (counted in the intro). Timestamps are turned into seconds via a Julian-day-number helper, so the arithmetic is portable. The distribution and the per-subscription averages/maxima re-aggregate under the date filter; click a subscription for its 10 most recent transfers. A CoreId takes the subscription of the first row in the group that carries one — every logical transfer has one, since a CoreId with no subscription at all is dropped from the cache.

Why the dwell matters

A file can arrive on time and still be late to the partner if it sits in the forward queue. Because that wait is invisible in both the inbound and outbound leg durations, a growing dwell is an early warning of queue pressure, a slow or backed-up outbound target, or a scheduling problem — well before it turns into an outright failure or an SLA breach. The per-subscription table pinpoints exactly which flow is slow to forward, and the p95/max tell you how bad the tail gets.
